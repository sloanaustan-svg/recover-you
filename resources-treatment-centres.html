Residential Addiction Treatment in Alberta

A plain-language guide to public programs, private treatment, inpatient care, and recovery communities

// Understanding Residential Addiction Treatment in Alberta

If you are searching for addiction treatment in Alberta, this page brings together a big-picture list of residential addiction treatment centres, inpatient recovery programs, publicly funded options, AHS-linked services, private treatment centres, and recovery communities across Calgary, Edmonton, Central Alberta, Northern Alberta, and Southern Alberta.

Not all treatment centres are built the same — and I wish someone had told me that early on. Some lean hard into 12-Step. Some are faith- or spiritually-based. Some are clinical and skills-focused. Some prioritize connection and community above everything else. Some are better suited for short-term stabilization, while others are long-term recovery communities. The differences matter.

This page is not here to sell you on one program. It is here to help you understand the landscape before you make a decision that affects your life, your family, your money, your time, and your recovery.

A few terms you'll see throughout this section. Different centres use different language — these definitions decode what you're looking at:

Jump to Treatment Centres

  • Quick Reference

    • RTC (Residential Treatment Centre): You live there full-time for the duration of treatment — meals, groups, curfews, the whole setup. This is what many people mean when they search for inpatient rehab or residential addiction treatment.
    • Inpatient Rehab: A common search term for live-in addiction treatment. In Alberta, programs may use terms like residential treatment, bed-based treatment, recovery community, or treatment centre instead.
    • PHP (Partial Hospitalization Program): A highly structured, full-day program you attend during the day but don't stay overnight.
    • IOP (Intensive Outpatient Program): Several treatment sessions per week — more flexible than PHP, but still structured and skills-focused.
    • OAT (Opioid Agonist Therapy): Medications such as Suboxone, Methadone, or Kadian used to stabilize opioid dependence and support recovery.
    • MAT (Medication-Assisted Treatment): A broader term that includes OAT plus other medications used to support addiction recovery, such as naltrexone or acamprosate.
    • 12-Step Based: Programs where AA/NA philosophy, meetings, sponsorship, step work, or Big Book teachings form a major part of the recovery model.
    • Trauma-Informed: Staff are trained to avoid re-traumatization and promote safety — not the same as trauma therapy.
    • Concurrent-Capable: Centres equipped to support addiction and mental health conditions at the same time. This matters if you are dealing with depression, anxiety, PTSD, ADHD, bipolar disorder, psychosis, or other mental health concerns alongside substance use.

    Wherever possible, I've included what each centre actually offers — length of stay, treatment style, population served, referral pathway, application notes, and what to ask before committing. The goal isn't to declare which place is best. It's to give you enough information to say: "This might fit me better than that."

    I've been direct elsewhere on this site about the ways many programs fall short on deep trauma work — even when they call themselves trauma-informed. That criticism stands. But there is something many programs can still do well: safety and stabilization. Treatment gives you a structured, contained environment long enough for your brain to clear, your body to regulate, and your energy to shift from "just surviving" to "okay, what's next?"

    Think of inpatient or residential addiction treatment as stabilization plus forward planning. No facility resolves a lifetime of addiction in 30–90 days — that's not an indictment of the system, it's just reality. But it can give you the footing to continue the deeper work once you leave.

    This section is here to help you:

    • Understand how different addiction treatment centres in Alberta operate — modality, population, length of stay, and referral process
    • Compare publicly funded treatment, private rehab, AHS-linked services, and recovery communities
    • Set realistic expectations around wait times, rules, cost, phone access, smoking policies, and daily structure
    • Identify what kind of environment and approach might actually work for you
    • Plan ahead so treatment becomes a launchpad into trauma-specific therapy, not a one-off event
    Before You Apply

    The five questions that come up most, answered plainly. How long the wait really is, what these programs actually differ on, how long you would be there, what daily life inside is like, and who pays.

    Wait Times for Addiction Treatment in Alberta

    Wait times are real, and they can be long. Weeks to months in some cases. That's brutal when you've finally reached the point of "I need help now."

    The system rarely meets us where we are in the exact moment we're ready. You get there, you reach out, and you find out it could be months — and that gap can be enough to make the whole thing feel pointless. If that's happened to you before, I get it.

    Here's the thing: that time is going to pass anyway. You might as well spend it moving up a list instead of wondering "what if."

    The most practical move? Get your applications in as soon as possible. It's almost always better to:

    • Put your name on multiple waitlists now
    • Complete intake paperwork while you're motivated
    • Call AHS, Recovery Alberta, or the treatment centre directly to confirm current intake steps
    • Be in a position where you might need to turn down a bed later, rather than have no options at all

    You are not wasting anyone's time by applying before you feel 100% ready. Getting on a list is how you give your future self options. You can always reassess when a bed opens.

    Run multiple applications at once — I recommend it. If a spot opens and it isn't your ideal program, take it seriously anyway; you can always transition if something better comes up later. Programs may not love this approach. I don't care — my aim is to get you the most informed path forward with the best odds of success.

    Where I can, I've included direct links to application pages. If you're not ready to do everything at once, that's fine. Today, maybe you just read through a couple that interest you and write their names down. Next time, you come back and review. After that, you fill one out and hit send. That's it. This page is here when you need it — piece by piece, or all at once.

    Different Treatment Centres, Different Recovery Models

    "Treatment" isn't one thing. Across Alberta you'll find:

    • Faith-based programs rooted in Christian or spiritual frameworks
    • 12-Step heavy programs where AA, NA, CA, meetings, sponsorship, or Big Book teachings form the core curriculum
    • Clinical models using counselling, CBT, DBT skills, relapse prevention, psychoeducation, and case planning
    • Culture- and land-based healing in Indigenous-led or Indigenous-serving programs
    • Recovery communities where peer support, shared accountability, daily structure, and connection are the primary therapeutic framework
    • Concurrent-disorder programs that are better equipped to support addiction and mental health concerns together

    I've flagged the main modalities for each centre — whether they're 12-Step oriented, faith-based, Indigenous-led, trauma-informed, concurrent-capable, skills-based, community-based, or clinically focused.

    None of these are automatically good or bad. The real question is: "Does this environment and philosophy line up with what I can actually engage in?" If you shut down around spiritual language, a heavily faith-based program may be a poor fit. If you thrive in ritual, structure, prayer, or ceremony, that same kind of program might be exactly what your nervous system needs.

    Most centres don't fully resolve trauma — but many can still do a solid job of stabilizing body, routine, thinking, and immediate risk so that trauma-specific work can actually land later.

    Remember: trauma-informed care ≠ trauma treatment. If you need trauma-specific therapy, ask directly whether it's offered. If it's not — which is common — use treatment for stabilization, then build a plan for dedicated trauma work after.

    How Long Is Residential Addiction Treatment?

    Across Alberta addiction treatment centres, you'll usually see:

    • Short-term programs: roughly 18–30 days
    • Medium-term programs: roughly 60–90 days
    • Long-term recovery communities: 6–12 months or more

    Honest recommendation — especially if your history includes trauma, repeated relapse, homelessness, unstable relationships, severe anxiety, or a long pattern of falling apart after brief periods of sobriety. Aim longer, not shorter.

    Thirty days is often just enough time to:

    • Stop shaking and sleeping like shit
    • Start eating normal meals and feeling semi-human
    • Get a small amount of clarity back
    • Begin noticing the emotional mess underneath the substance use

    And then you're dropped back into the exact environment that kept you sick.

    If you can manage it, 90 days is a much better baseline. It gives you time to:

    • Stabilize physically and emotionally
    • Actually absorb the material instead of just surviving withdrawal
    • Notice patterns that don't show up in the first two weeks
    • Build a discharge plan before panic takes over
    • Map out next steps before you leave — therapy, trauma-focused treatment, housing, support, meetings, outpatient care, or medical follow-up

    Going to treatment affects your life and the people around you. If you're going to hit pause, let it be long enough to matter. Overestimating the time you need is almost always safer than underestimating it and ending up right back where you started.

    What Inpatient Rehab Feels Like: Rules, Routine & Structure

    Every centre is different, and I'm not going to label programs as good or bad based on vibe alone. A lot of that is subjective, and online reviews are usually written by people in extreme emotion — which tells you more about their state than about the full picture of the program.

    That said, some things are consistent across most structured residential addiction treatment programs:

    • Limited or no cell phone access — especially early on
    • Strict rules around passes, visitors, and outings
    • Curfews, schedules, and group attendance are usually non-negotiable
    • Chores and community responsibilities — cleaning, dishes, shared upkeep
    • Group work, even when you don't feel like talking
    • Consequences if you break rules, leave without approval, bring substances on-site, or use during the program
    • No Smoking / Vaping / Nicotine Pouches. Many AHS facilities are smoke-free, including vaping, and some programs are moving toward fully nicotine-free environments. Not universal — call ahead to confirm. Patches, lozenges, and nicotine sprays may be available on-site.

      Please don't let this be the thing that stops you. I know exactly what it feels like to clock a no-smoking sign and start scanning for the exit. Don't walk away from life-saving care because you can't spark a dart in the parking lot.

    It can feel like having your wings clipped. In many cases, that's exactly what's needed for a while. You're not there to prove you can live independently. You're there to get stable enough to build something different when you leave.

    The structure is not the whole solution. But when your life has been chaos, structure can become the first container strong enough to hold you while you get your footing back.

    Cost & Funding: Is Addiction Treatment Free in Alberta?

    For the programs listed on this page, many AHS-operated, AHS-contracted, Recovery Alberta-linked, or publicly funded treatment centres are low-cost or no-cost for eligible Alberta residents. That typically means:

    • No program fee for the core treatment stay in many publicly funded programs
    • No private insurance required for admission to publicly funded treatment
    • Access may still require an Alberta Health Care number, referral, income support approval, or confirmation of eligibility
    • Some recovery houses or transitional programs may still involve room and board, rent, or monthly fees

    Some programs on this list are semi-private, recovery housing, post-treatment residences, or transitional living programs, where:

    • The treatment portion may be funded
    • Room and board may be covered through AISH, Alberta Works, other income supports, or a modest monthly fee
    • Funding rules may depend on income, program type, referral pathway, or eligibility criteria

    Fully private rehab centres are a different story. Fee-for-service, often thousands per month. If someone is asking for large up-front payments, you're dealing with the private system — not a standard publicly funded treatment bed.

    When you call, ask directly:

    • "Is your program publicly funded?"
    • "Are there any fees I need to plan for?"
    • "How are room and board handled?"
    • "Do I need Alberta Health Care coverage?"
    • "Do I need a referral from AHS, Recovery Alberta, a doctor, detox, or an addiction counsellor?"
    • "Can I apply myself, or does a clinician need to apply for me?"

    Money is a real barrier. It shouldn't be the thing that stops you from getting treatment. There are more funded addiction treatment options in Alberta than most people realize — you just have to know where to look and what to ask.

    Treatment isn't a magic reset. It's a contained space where, for a period of time, your only real job is to stay, show up, and let your system stabilize enough to think clearly again.

    You don't have to love the rules, the schedule, or every group session. You don't have to feel ready every day. You just have to keep not leaving.

    The goal is simple: walk out with more stability than you walked in with, and with at least one concrete next step lined up — especially if that step involves trauma-focused therapy, outpatient support, housing, medical care, peer support, or a safer daily routine.

    How to Use This List

    These are residential and inpatient addiction treatment programs across Alberta. Unlike detox, treatment addresses the psychological, behavioral, and social roots of substance use — typically over weeks or months, not days.

    Programs vary widely in approach: abstinence-based and 12-Step models, concurrent mental health care, structured recovery communities, and more. Length of stay, eligibility, and referral pathways differ by program. Confirm current wait times, referral requirements, and funding eligibility directly with the program before taking any steps.

    This list is organized by region and prioritizes clarity over promotion.

    About this list:
    Every listing has been manually sourced and reviewed, with details drawn from official websites, application forms, phone contacts, and publicly available materials. Where some entries are more detailed than others, this reflects the availability of verifiable information — not a lack of effort. Where something is unclear or unsupported, it is simplified or omitted intentionally.

    A note on trauma:
    None of the programs listed are endorsed by Recover-You as trauma-specific treatment providers based on publicly available information. This does not mean trauma goes unaddressed — only that clear evidence of trauma-focused treatment methods was not found.

    Some listings note where language such as "trauma-informed" or support for co-occurring disorders (including PTSD) was identified — but no specific trauma-focused modalities were confirmed in the available information.

    If trauma treatment matters to you, ask direct questions before committing: what specific therapies are used, how trauma is processed, and what training staff have in this area. Speaking from personal experience, the "trauma-informed" label is applied loosely, and finding out it meant next to nothing — after you've already committed — is devastating. Many programs are not equipped to provide dedicated trauma treatment. Clarify this before you go in.

    If you spot an error, outdated detail, or gap in coverage, or know of a program that should be listed, get in touch.

      Claresholm Centre for Mental Health & Addictions (CCMHA)

      South Zone

      Why This Program Is Highlighted:
      Claresholm is one of the few facilities in Alberta offering fully integrated inpatient treatment for co-occurring addiction and mental health conditions. (Note: This program is sometimes confused with the nearby Lander Treatment Centre, a separate facility also listed below.)

      By the time someone reaches residential-level care, presenting with both a substance use disorder and a mental health condition is common. When these conditions are treated in isolation, care can become fragmented and stabilization incomplete. Programs that integrate both areas within a single setting offer a more coordinated approach to treatment and discharge planning.

      Note:
      This is one of the few programs in Alberta where mental health and addiction are treated together within the same inpatient setting, rather than through separate or sequential services.

      Description:
      CCMHA is a specialized inpatient facility providing integrated care for adults with co-occurring addiction and mental health conditions. Medical, psychiatric, and therapeutic services are delivered within a coordinated residential program.

      Demographic:
      Adults (18+)
      (Co-ed)

      Modality:

      • Integrated addiction and mental health treatment
      • Comprehensive psychiatric care
      • Medical and nursing support
      • Individual and group therapy
      • Psychoeducation and life-skills programming
      • Structured inpatient programming
      • Coordinated discharge and continuing care planning

      Program Structure:

      • Concurrent Disorders Department: Short-term inpatient treatment focused on stabilization, recovery skills, and transition planning.
      • Active Rehabilitation Department: Medium-term inpatient rehabilitation for individuals with ongoing mental health needs.
      • Extended Rehabilitation Department: Longer-term inpatient care for individuals requiring sustained support and stabilization.
      Call Website
