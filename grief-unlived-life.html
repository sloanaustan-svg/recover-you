The Grief No One Sends Flowers For

Grieving what never was, and what was taken before it had a chance to be.

Not All Grief Comes With a Funeral

When someone dies, the world knows what to do. There's a service. Food shows up at the door. People lower their voices around you, and for a little while everyone agrees that you've lost something real.

But some of the heaviest grief a person can carry never gets any of that. No obituary. No cards. No one pulling you aside to ask how you're holding up. Because what you lost wasn't a person in a casket. It was a childhood that should have been safe. A parent who was there but never really present. Years that disappeared into survival, and then into addiction. A version of yourself that never got the chance to exist.

That grief is every bit as real. It just doesn't come with flowers.

You can't bury what never got to live.

So most of us carry it instead. Usually without a name for it. Often for decades.

This page covers both kinds of loss: the ones everyone recognizes, and the ones almost nobody does. Death, survivor's guilt, the people we've buried. But mostly the quieter losses. The loss of innocence. The loss of what could have been. And the uncomfortable place where two hard truths meet: what happened to you was not fair, and what you do with it now is still yours.

If you've come here from Shame and the False Self, this is the other half of that conversation. Shame says you are the problem. Grief says something was lost. Only one of those is true.

// What Grief Actually Is

Grief isn't a malfunction. It's what attachment feels like when the thing you're attached to is gone, or was never there to begin with.

Neuroscientist and grief researcher Mary-Frances O'Connor describes grief as a kind of learning problem. The brain keeps a map of the people we're bonded to: that they exist, that they'll come back, that they'll be there tomorrow. When someone dies, that map doesn't update overnight. Part of you knows they're gone. Another part keeps expecting them to walk through the door. Grieving is the slow, painful work of the brain learning a reality it never wanted to learn.

The same logic applies to losses that never involved a death. If you spent your childhood waiting for a parent to become safe, part of your brain may still be waiting. The map was drawn around someone who was supposed to show up. They didn't. And the map never got the update.

Forget the Five Stages

Denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance. Almost everyone has heard of them, and almost everyone assumes grief is supposed to move through them in order. It doesn't. Elisabeth Kübler-Ross built those stages from her work with people who were dying, not people who were grieving, and the research since has found that most bereaved people simply don't move through grief in tidy steps. In 2017, grief researchers Margaret Stroebe, Henk Schut and Kathrin Boerner went as far as warning health-care professionals that the stage model can do harm, by teaching people they're grieving "wrong" whenever their experience doesn't match the script.

What the evidence supports instead looks more like a pendulum. Stroebe and Schut's Dual Process Model describes healthy grieving as oscillation: time spent facing the loss, and time spent stepping away from it to rebuild a life. Both are part of the work. Stepping away from grief isn't avoidance. It's part of how grieving works.

  • Grief doesn't keep a schedule. It comes in waves, often set off by things you didn't see coming.
  • Feeling okay for a while doesn't mean you're finished.
  • Feeling it again doesn't mean you've gone backwards.

// The Losses Everyone Recognizes

Before getting to the grief nobody talks about, it's worth naming the grief everybody does. For people with trauma and addiction in their history, even ordinary grief rarely arrives in an ordinary way.

When Someone Dies

Losing someone you love is hard on anyone. For a nervous system already running on high alert, it can be destabilizing in ways that are difficult to explain to people who don't share that wiring. A death confirms the oldest rule trauma ever taught you: the people you need can disappear, and there is nothing you can do about it.

And for a lot of us, the substance was how we survived feelings that size. A large national study of bereaved adults found that men in the first two years after losing a close family member showed markedly higher rates of harmful drinking than men who hadn't lost anyone. If you're in recovery, a death can be one of the most dangerous stretches you'll face. Not because you're weak, but because grief is exactly the kind of pain the substance used to manage.

When the Death Comes With Stigma

Overdose deaths, alcohol-related deaths and suicides tend to arrive wrapped in silence. People don't know what to say, so they say nothing. Or worse, they say something about choices. Researchers who studied families bereaved by drug and alcohol deaths described what they found as a disenfranchised grief: the loss is just as real, but the stigma attached to how the person died makes it harder to mourn openly, and harder for anyone to offer comfort.

If you've lost someone to addiction, your grief deserves exactly the same room as anyone else's. They were never just their cause of death.

When the Person Who Died Is the Person Who Hurt You

This one rarely gets talked about. What do you do with grief for a parent who was also the source of your pain? Some people feel nothing, then feel guilty for feeling nothing. Some are flattened by sadness and can't understand why they're mourning someone who did so much damage. Some feel relief, and then shame for the relief.

All of it is normal. When someone who hurt you dies, you aren't only grieving them. You're grieving the possibility that they would ever change, apologize, or become the person you needed. Death closes the door on repair, and that is its own loss.

// A Personal Story

My Mother

My mother died at fifty. Addiction followed her for her whole life, and I was her last child. She held things mostly together until I was born. Then it all fell apart.

There was never a real point of connection between us. What followed was a childhood no child should have. As hard as that was, the deepest scar wasn't any single thing that happened to me. It was feeling rejected by my own mother, and having her watch it happen without stepping in.

My grief for her moved in a way no stage model would predict. Desperate for her love. Confused about where I fit. Certain I didn't fit anywhere. Then hate. Then I shut it out completely. I told everyone I was fine, that I didn't think about it anymore. I didn't understand yet that not thinking about something and building walls around it are not the same thing. Behind those walls, a belief took hold before I had language for it: that I was broken, that I was what was wrong. My addiction became the evidence.

What changed it was understanding. My own addiction taught me something I never wanted to learn: you can love someone deeply and still choose to hurt them, every time you reach for the relief instead of them. That opened the door to empathy. Then I learned what addiction does to a person, and I learned about her own childhood, which was horrific. She didn't hate me. She was in pain her whole life and medicated it the only way she could, with no map to healing. Once addiction moved in, her maternal instincts were gone. She wasn't acting out of malice. She was acting out of pain she never understood.

That understanding didn't change what happened. It changed what I carry. The hate became empathy, and then love.

Her story and mine are the architecture behind everything on this site. When she was my age, there were no real tools. There was “get over it.” There was “that didn't happen.” Without that knowledge, a lot of people before us were doomed to repeat what was done to them. Now we know better. The education I found and the science we have now are what let me break the intergenerational cycle. Those of us still suffering deserve every one of those tools. She never got them. That's what this site is for.

// Survivor's Guilt

“Why do I get another chance when they didn't?”

If you've been in recovery for any length of time, you probably know someone who didn't make it. Someone you used with. Someone who sat beside you in treatment. Someone who was doing well, until suddenly they weren't. In the middle of an overdose crisis, a lot of people in recovery are carrying more of these losses than they will ever say out loud.

Survivor's guilt is the sense that your survival is undeserved, or worse, that it somehow came at someone else's expense. It rarely shows up as a clear thought. Much more often it shows up in behaviour:

  • Hiding or downplaying milestones in sobriety, because celebrating feels disrespectful to the people who died.
  • Sabotaging a job, a relationship or a stretch of stability right when things start going well, as if you aren't allowed a better life.
  • Holding back in therapy because some part of you doesn't believe you've earned the right to get better.
  • Living as though you owe a debt you can never finish paying.

Put that list next to the Shame page and you'll notice how much they overlap. Survivor's guilt often tangles grief together with shame. The loss is real. The verdict attached to it is not.

// The Reframe

Your life is not a debt to the people who didn't make it. If it's anything, it's the chance they didn't get. The most meaningful thing you can do with it is the one thing they can't: live it, fully, and stop apologizing for still being here.

The thing to hold onto:

You don't have to earn your survival. You only have to stop refusing it.

// Ambiguous Grief: Grieving What Never Was

Some losses never have an ending. No death certificate. No date to mark. Nothing anyone else can see. Family therapist and researcher Pauline Boss spent decades studying losses like these and gave them a name: ambiguous loss.

Boss described two kinds. In the first, someone is physically gone but still psychologically present: a person who went missing, a parent who walked out and never came back. In the second, someone is physically present but psychologically gone. They're in the room, at the dinner table, in the family photos, but the person you needed isn't really there. Boss's own list of examples for that second kind includes addiction.

For a lot of trauma survivors, that second kind describes an entire childhood. And because nothing visibly ended, nobody ever told you that you'd lost something. Psychotherapist Francis Weller names one of grief's "gates" as what we expected and did not receive. It might be the most accurate description of childhood trauma ever written in five words. You can't mourn a loss you were never allowed to see.

Here is what that grief can look like:

The Childhood You Didn't Get Loss of innocence

Childhood is supposed to be the one stretch of life where someone else carries the weight. If you spent it watching the adults for danger, managing a parent's moods or learning to be invisible, you lost something most people take for granted: the chance to just be a kid. That isn't nostalgia. It's a real loss, and it's allowed to hurt.

The Parent You Needed Present, but absent

There's the parent you had and the parent you needed, and for a lot of us they were never the same person. Grieving that gap doesn't require them to be gone. They might still be alive. They might still call on Sundays. You can love them and still grieve who they never became.

Who You Might Have Been The unlived self

The person you could have become with safety, support, and a nervous system that wasn't built for war. The talents nobody encouraged. The confidence that never had a chance to form. The life that was interrupted before it ever started. More on this one below.

The Lost Years Time you can't get back

Years spent surviving. Years lost to using. The moments you missed, the relationships that didn't make it through, the people who moved on while you were stuck. Counting them can feel unbearable. Refusing to count them doesn't bring them back.

Here, but Gone Addiction, from both sides

Loving someone in active addiction is ambiguous loss in its purest form. They're alive. They might be in the next room. And you grieve them anyway. If you were the one using, the people who loved you were carrying this too, often for years.

The Substance Itself Losing the thing that worked

It sounds strange to say out loud, but for many of us the substance was the most reliable relationship we had. It showed up every time. When it's gone, what remains isn't just absence. It's grief, for what it provided, and for what trauma took before it ever arrived.

The World You Used In People you didn't perform for

Recovery often means walking away from people, places and a version of yourself that, for all the damage, felt like home. For years the only people I didn't have to perform for were the ones I drank with. Leaving that behind was the right call. It was also a loss.

The Mask The self you built to survive

Even the false self is something you grieve. You wore it for decades. It got you through. Letting it go, even when you know you have to, can feel like losing the only version of you that ever worked.

Boss is blunt about what this kind of grief needs. Not closure. Ambiguous losses rarely give you a clean ending to grieve, so waiting for one keeps you frozen. The goal is learning to carry the loss in a way that lets you keep living. You don't have to resolve it to stop being run by it.

// Grieving Who You Might Have Been

When most people think of grief, they think of death. But for trauma survivors there's another kind, quieter and in some ways harder: grieving the unrealized version of you that never came to be. The life that was interrupted before it ever had a chance to start.

This pain doesn't heal like a broken bone. It lingers, and left unwitnessed, it can consume you. For years I carried resentment toward what happened to me. If I'm honest, some of it was closer to hate. But eventually I had to accept what couldn't be undone: what could have been never was, and what remains is all that matters now.

What surprised me was this: allowing myself to grieve didn't weaken me. It stabilized me. Once I stopped fighting the sadness, it stopped running my life. The grief didn't disappear, but it changed shape, from something that was happening to me into something I was moving through.

This kind of grief also has a habit of arriving late. It shows up when you watch someone else's kid get the encouragement you never got. When a friend talks easily about calling their dad for advice. When you finally get some stability in recovery and realize how much of your life was lived without it. Healing often makes this grief louder before it makes it quieter, because for the first time you're safe enough to see what was missing.

If this is touching somewhere tender, that's okay. It means something in here recognized something in you. You don't have to sit with it alone. If you need to talk to someone right now, crisis support is available here, and if you want to explore what longer-term help might look like, the resources page has options worth knowing about.

// Grief Without Permission

In 1989, grief researcher Kenneth Doka coined the term disenfranchised grief for grief that isn't openly acknowledged, socially supported or publicly mourned. The loss doesn't count in the eyes of the people around you, so you carry it without the rituals, the sympathy, or the time off that other grief gets.

Almost every loss on this page fits that description. Nobody sends a card when you finally realize your childhood wasn't safe. There's no bereavement leave for the decade you lost to addiction. And sometimes the people who should understand best are the ones who shut it down fastest:

  • “That was a long time ago.”
  • “At least you're sober now.”
  • “Your parents did the best they could.”
  • “Everything happens for a reason.”
  • “Just be grateful for what you have.”

Some of those might even be partly true. But every one of them closes the door on grief before it's had a chance to move through. Recovery culture can be especially good at this. Gratitude is a real tool. Used as a muzzle, it just teaches people to perform being okay, which is exactly what most of us were doing before we got here.

On the “No Regrets” Cliché

People who've spent time getting to know me often ask, "Do you wish your life had been different?" What they're really asking, what they usually mean, is "Do you wish none of it had ever happened to you?"

I know the approved recovery answer. I've heard it a thousand times: "I wouldn't change a thing. My struggle made me who I am. No regrets." And look, I get why people say it. I do. It's tidy. It's hopeful. It makes for a good bumper sticker.

But if you're asking me to be honest? Hell yes, I wish things had been different. I wish I hadn't suffered. I wish the people who were supposed to protect me had. I wish I hadn't lost decades to something that started before I was old enough to understand what was happening to me. In my experience, that kind of peace usually comes after the grief, not instead of it.

The brutal reality is that it happened anyway. No amount of wishing, bargaining or reframing will ever rewrite it. What matters now, the only thing that matters now, is what we do with the time we have left. For me, true strength isn't pretending the damage was a gift. It's recognizing the miracle of the humanity I managed to hold onto in spite of it.

// Two Things Are True

It wasn't fair. And it's still yours.

There's a point in grief where most people get stuck, and it's usually on one side of a line. Some never let themselves feel how unfair it was. They skip straight to "move on," "let it go," "it made me who I am." Others feel the unfairness so completely that it becomes the whole story, and moving forward starts to feel like letting the people who hurt them off the hook.

Healing asks you to stand on both sides of that line at once.

The First Truth

It Happened, and It Wasn't Fair

You didn't choose the house you grew up in. You didn't choose the nervous system it built, the beliefs it installed, or the way your brain learned to treat a substance like oxygen. Nobody asked a child for consent. That isn't a technicality. It is an injustice, and it deserves to be named as one.

Honouring that means letting it be as big as it actually was. Acknowledging it out loud, even if only to yourself. Feeling the anger, the sadness and the bitterness without rushing to tidy them up for anyone else's comfort. It means you stop shrinking what happened so other people can stay comfortable.

What this truth protects

Your story, from being rewritten as your fault. Skip this part and the injustice doesn't go anywhere. It goes underground and comes back as shame.

The Second Truth

It's Still Ours to Carry Forward

This is the part nobody wants to hear. It isn't fair that the work of healing falls on the people who were hurt. It falls on us anyway. The people responsible for the damage are rarely the ones who show up to repair it. Many can't. Some are gone. Waiting for them to make it right keeps your life on hold until someone else decides it's allowed to start.

Responsibility isn't blame. It doesn't mean any of it was your fault. It means you're the only one who can decide where it goes from here. That isn't a punishment. For a lot of us, it's the first real power we've ever had.

What this truth protects

Your future, from being held hostage by your past. Skip this part and grief stops being something you move through. It becomes somewhere you live.

// Both at Once

Holding two opposing truths at the same time isn't a contradiction. It's the skill. Pauline Boss calls it both/and thinking, and argues it's how people lower the stress of losses that can never be fully resolved. DBT builds the same muscle through radical acceptance: accepting reality completely, not because it's okay, but because fighting it keeps you stuck inside it.

In practice the order tends to matter. Most people can't take ownership of their recovery until they've stopped taking ownership of what was done to them. That's the righteous indignation piece. The grief comes after it. And once the grief has had its say, moving forward stops feeling like a betrayal of the kid who went through it.

It wasn't your fault. It is your responsibility. Grief is what makes both of those bearable.

// Where Grief and Addiction Meet

If grief is the pain of attachment meeting loss, addiction often moves into the space grief was supposed to occupy.

In 2008, a UCLA team led by Mary-Frances O'Connor scanned the brains of bereaved women while they were shown reminders of the person they'd lost. In the women whose grief had stayed raw and unrelenting, those reminders lit up the nucleus accumbens, a core part of the brain's reward system, and the more they yearned for the person, the stronger that activation was. The researchers titled their paper "Craving love?" for a reason. It was a small study, so it shows an overlap, not a cause. But grief that can't move forward can involve the same reward circuitry as craving.

That doesn't mean grief causes addiction. It means the two can feel strangely alike from the inside: the longing, the preoccupation, the sense that something essential is missing and you'd do almost anything to get it back. For a lot of us, the substance was the first thing that ever seemed to answer that ache.

It may also be part of why sobriety so often uncovers grief instead of resolving it. Take away the thing that numbed the loss, and the loss is still there, now with nothing standing in front of it. If early recovery has felt like drowning in a sadness you can't explain, this is a big part of why sobriety can feel worse before it feels better.

Mourning Is Part of Healing, Not a Detour

Judith Herman's model of trauma recovery, the backbone of most trauma-informed care today, has three stages: safety and stabilization, remembrance and mourning, and reconnection. Mourning isn't something that gets in the way of recovery. It's the middle of it. It's also why grief work is built directly into the TFR Model. Herman herself was clear that the stages overlap and rarely run in a straight line.

Pacing matters. Grief work started before there's enough stability to hold it can overwhelm a person and push them back toward the very things they're trying to leave. Not every loss needs digging into. But if one keeps showing up in your recovery, as irritability, numbness or cravings, it's asking for attention.

Safety first. Then grief, at a pace you can hold.

// So, What About You? Name What You've Lost

Grief that has a name is easier to carry than grief that doesn't. Most of us have never been asked to list what we lost, because most of it never looked like loss to anyone else. Read through these slowly. Tap any that are yours.

There's no score here and no right number. Naming one loss honestly matters more than marking twenty.

// What you named

The losses you carry

This isn't a diagnosis and it isn't a measure of how much you've suffered. It's a list of things that deserved to be grieved, maybe for the first time, in your own hands.

Nothing you mark here is saved, sent or tracked. It lives in this page and disappears when you close it. Copy or print it if you want to keep it, write one of these losses into an unsendable letter, or bring the list to a trauma-informed therapist as a place to start.

// Moving Through It

There's no way around grief. There's only through, and through doesn't mean over. The goal isn't to stop feeling the loss. It's to carry it in a way that no longer runs your life.

Pete Walker, whose work on complex trauma runs through much of this site, puts grieving at the centre of recovering from childhood trauma. He describes it working through four channels: angering, crying, verbal ventilating and feeling. Most of us were taught early that all four were dangerous. Letting them move again is a big part of the work.

Grief also needs a witness. Herman's work makes the point again and again that trauma recovery happens in relationship, not in isolation, and grief is no different. Saying it out loud to someone who doesn't flinch, a therapist, a friend, a room full of people who've lost what you've lost, does something that thinking about it alone never will.

And you don't have to do it all at once. Remember the pendulum. Time with the grief, then time away from it. Both count.

Give It Shape

  • Write an unsendable letter to the person you lost, the parent you needed, or the kid you were.
  • Mark your losses on Chart Your Life. Seeing them in sequence can explain things nothing else could.
  • Make a small ritual. A date, a place, an object. Grief without ritual tends to find its own, less healthy ones.

Let It Move

  • Keep a bond where it helps. Staying connected to who or what you lost, through memory, meaning or something you carry forward, can be part of healthy grief.
  • Find people who know this loss. Grief shared with someone who gets it weighs differently.
  • Let the anger count. Anger is one of grief's channels, not a sign you're doing it wrong.

Protect Your Recovery

  • Treat anniversaries, holidays and funerals as high-risk days. Plan for them ahead of time.
  • Tell someone when a loss hits. Grief carried in isolation is where relapse tends to live.
  • If the urge to use rises with the grief, that isn't failure. It's information. Reach for a regulation tool before you reach for anything else.
When grief gets stuck

Most grief, even devastating grief, gradually softens. For some people it doesn't. In 2022 the DSM-5-TR added Prolonged Grief Disorder: grief after a death that, a year or more later (six months for children and teens), still involves intense yearning for or preoccupation with the person nearly every day, plus at least three other symptoms such as feeling part of you died too, emotional numbness or intense loneliness. It also has to cause real distress or disrupt daily life beyond what's expected in your culture or faith. Missing someone intensely a year later is not, on its own, a disorder.

It isn't a character flaw, and it's treatable. If that sounds like you, it's worth talking to a professional who understands both grief and trauma. The trauma therapy resources page is a good place to start, and DSM vs ICD explains what a diagnosis like this does and doesn't mean.

Grief Support in Canada and Alberta
  • MyGrief.ca: online grief modules from Canadian Virtual Hospice, built by grief specialists alongside people who've lived through loss.
  • Hospice Calgary: grief counselling and support groups for children, teens and adults.
  • Moms Stop the Harm: peer support for families who've lost someone to substance use (Healing Hearts) or who love someone who's still using (Holding Hope).
  • Health Link 811: 24/7 health advice and referrals anywhere in Alberta.
  • 988: call or text, 24/7, if grief has turned into thoughts of not wanting to be here.
Grieving What Never Was

What Remains

You can't go back and give yourself the childhood you deserved. You can't get the years back. You can't turn the people who hurt you into the people you needed, and you can't bring back the ones who didn't make it.

What you can do is stop pretending none of it mattered. Let it be as unfair as it was. Feel it all the way through. Then pick up what's left, which is more than it looks like from inside the grief, and build something with it.

Grief isn't proof that you're broken. It's proof that something in you always knew what you deserved.

Honour what was lost. Then live like what remains is worth it. Because it is.
